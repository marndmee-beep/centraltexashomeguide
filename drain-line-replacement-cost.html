Drain line replacement cost

The drain lines under a 1970s or 1980s Central Texas house are often original cast iron, and cast iron eventually rusts through. Here's what replacing them costs.

Advertisement

The short answer

Drain line replacement runs $80 to $200 per linear foot, so a typical home with 40 to 60 feet of main line lands between $4,000 and $11,000. Trenchless replacement, where a new liner or pipe is pulled through the old one, costs $6,000 to $15,000 but saves the yard and the floors.

Why cast iron fails here

Cast iron drain lines were standard from the 1950s into the 1980s. Inside the pipe, waste and hard water deposits build a crust, and the pipe corrodes from the inside out. In this region the combination of hard water and the heat of an attic run accelerates that. By 40 or 50 years, many cast iron lines are cracked, sagging or root-invaded.

Get the camera first. Never accept a replacement quote without seeing a camera survey of your own line. It shows the exact location and extent of the damage, and it's the difference between a $2,000 spot repair and an $11,000 full replacement.

Signs of a failing drain line

  • Slow drains throughout the house, not just one fixture.
  • Repeat clogs in the same place, especially after a professional clearing.
  • Sewage smell in the yard or in the house, particularly after rain.
  • Soggy patches in the lawn over the line, or unusually green grass in a strip.
  • Gurgling toilets when other fixtures drain.
  • Backups into a shower or tub when the washing machine empties.

Spot repair, replacement, or trenchless?

  • One damaged section on otherwise sound pipe: a spot repair is sensible and much cheaper.
  • Widespread cracking or several failed sections: full replacement is the honest answer, because the rest of the pipe is the same age.
  • Line running under a finished floor, a driveway or mature landscaping: trenchless avoids destroying what's above it.
  • Roots entering at joints, repeatedly: cutting roots clears the line temporarily. Without repairing the joint, they return.
Where people get oversold: a contractor who quotes a full trenchless replacement off a single clog, before running a camera. Ask to see the footage, ask how many feet are damaged, and get a second opinion on anything over $5,000.

What affects your price

  • Depth. Deeper lines mean more excavation and shoring.
  • Access. A line under a slab, driveway or established tree costs more than one in open yard.
  • What's above it. Tile floors, cabinetry and landscaping all add cost to put back.
  • Length. Distance to the city main or septic tank drives the total.
  • Permits. Most Central Texas jurisdictions require one for drain line work.

Questions to ask

  • Will you run a camera survey and show me the footage before quoting?
  • How many feet are actually damaged, and how many feet are you proposing to replace?
  • Is the price for open trench or trenchless, and why that choice?
  • Who repairs the floor, driveway or lawn afterwards, and is that in the price?
  • What warranty comes with the new line?
  • Should I involve my homeowners insurer? Many policies cover sudden damage but not gradual deterioration, so ask your own insurer.
