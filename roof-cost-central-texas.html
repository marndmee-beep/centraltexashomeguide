Roof replacement cost in Central Texas

Shingle prices per square, why roofs here age faster than the national average, and how hail claims actually work.

The short answer

Replacing an asphalt shingle roof on a typical 1,800 to 2,200 square foot Central Texas home runs $9,000 to $18,000 installed. That's roughly $450 to $750 per square (a square is 100 square feet of roof), including tear-off, underlayment, flashing, ridge vent and disposal.

Why roofs wear out faster here

  • UV intensity. Central Texas gets far more sun than the national average. Ultraviolet light breaks down the asphalt in shingles, so a roof rated for 25 years may last 15 to 18 here.
  • Thermal cycling. Surface temperatures on a roof can swing 60 degrees between day and night. Expansion and contraction loosens granules and opens seams.
  • Hail. The I-35 corridor is one of the most hail-prone regions in the country. A single storm can bruise shingles across a whole neighbourhood.
  • Wind and blown debris. Spring storm systems lift shingle edges, and once water gets under them, damage spreads quickly.

The counterintuitive point on metal

Metal roofing costs two to three times more up front, and in most of the country the payback calculation is unconvincing. Central Texas is one of the places where it's closer to working: the roof lasts twice as long, it reflects solar heat which lowers cooling bills, and it resists hail far better than shingles. If you plan to stay in the house more than 15 years, ask for a metal quote alongside the shingle ones.

Before you sign anything: ask whether the price includes replacing rotten decking, and at what rate per sheet. That's the single most common surprise on a roofing invoice, and it usually appears after the old roof is off.

Hail claims

Most Central Texas roof replacements are partly or wholly paid by insurance. The sequence matters:

  • Document before you call anyone. Photograph the roof if you can do so safely, and note the date of the storm.
  • File the claim, then get contractor quotes. Getting quotes first is fine, but signing a contract before the insurer has assessed it can complicate the claim.
  • Know your deductible. Texas wind and hail deductibles are often a percentage of the home's insured value, not a flat dollar amount.
  • Avoid anyone who offers to "handle the whole claim" or waives your deductible. In Texas, a contractor cannot legally pay or waive your insurance deductible. That offer is a warning sign.
